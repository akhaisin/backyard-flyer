# PD vs PID — two controllers, side by side

Two floaters fly the same flight pattern mirrored across the X axis. The **left, blue** floater is driven by a **PD** controller (proportional + damping). The **right, orange** floater is driven by a full **PID** controller. Everything else — hardware model, physics, waypoint spacing — is identical.

The mirror means both vehicles step through the same Y and Z schedule, so the charts show two controllers tracking the *same* targets, just with a sign flip on X.

## Why not pure P?

Before getting into the comparison: why not start with a pure proportional controller — `thrust = K · error` and nothing else?

Because **pure P doesn't converge**. With no term that dissipates energy, the floater behaves like a frictionless spring: it accelerates toward the target, overshoots, decelerates on the far side, accelerates back, and oscillates forever. The waypoint threshold (1.5 m) never catches a stable approach, and the demo never progresses past waypoint 0.

There's a familiar example of this exact failure mode at planetary scale:

> The Moon orbits the Earth indefinitely. Earth's gravity is, conceptually, a "pure P" controller — a restoring force toward a target point, with no damping at all. There's no friction in space, so no energy is dissipated, and the Moon traces the same loop forever. Satellites in low Earth orbit *do* eventually fall — but only because the thin upper atmosphere provides a tiny amount of damping. Without it, they'd orbit forever too.

So the simplest controller that can actually settle on a waypoint needs at least one dissipative term. The blue floater uses the minimal addition: a derivative term applied as `−KV · velocity`, which pulls back against motion. That's PD.

## See them fly

Press **Start** and watch the two trajectories develop in parallel.

- The blue floater (**PD**) settles near each waypoint with visible overshoot before the velocity term reins it in.
- The orange floater (**PID**) damps harder and uses an integral term, so it settles smoothly with little overshoot.
- The cyan **wind arrow** at the top of the scene shows the current gust — direction matches a horizontal compass, length grows with magnitude.

**What to watch over time:**

1. **Settle.** After the first lap or two, the orange (PID) trajectory locks in tight around its waypoints. The integral has caught up to the steady-state wind and pre-loads the right counter-thrust each tick. PD stays a step behind — it has no memory of the disturbance and gets nudged downwind on every approach.
2. **Strong gust.** Watch for the wind arrow to lengthen sharply and swing to a new direction. Both floaters get knocked off course — PID more than you'd expect, because its integral was tuned for the *previous* gust and now points the wrong way. There's a brief window where PD actually looks better.
3. **Re-stabilise.** Within ~1–2 seconds PID's integral has unwound the stale bias and re-accumulated for the new wind. The orange trajectory snaps back to its tight pattern; PD remains visibly worse.

That cycle — *settle, perturbation, recover* — is exactly the loop real autopilots run continuously. The lap counter chart is the cleanest summary of the net effect.

## How it works

Both floaters share the same four-block chain introduced by the [Floater model](#sim-demo/floater):

```
mission → fc → hw → world
```

`hw` and `world` are byte-identical between the two vehicles — same engine, same physics. Only `mission` (the waypoint table) and `fc` (the control law) differ.

### PD (`fc_pd.ts`)

```
thrust = KP · (target − pos) − KV · velocity
```

- **Proportional** (`KP=3.0`): the main pull toward the target.
- **Derivative** (`KV=0.5`, applied as `−KV · velocity`): pulls against motion, dissipating the energy that pure P would let bounce around forever.

This is the *minimum viable* controller. It converges, but it overshoots and leaves a small steady-state error (gravity is compensated as a constant force, but any miscalibration accumulates as drift).

### PID (`fc_pid.ts`)

```
thrust = KP · error + KI · ∫error · dt + KD · d(error)/dt
```

This is *pure* PID — no mass, no gravity feedforward. Unlike PD, the PID controller is not told that `MASS · GRAVITY` of upward thrust is needed to hold altitude. Whatever the floater needs to fight gravity has to come out of the **integral** term as it accumulates the constant error.

- **Integral**: closes steady-state error. Here it has the harder job of discovering gravity on its own — note `MAX_INT=15`, so the integral can contribute at most `KI · MAX_INT = 4.5 N`, less than the `9.81 N` needed to hover. The floater will tend to sag below each y-target until the proportional term re-engages.
- **Derivative**: here operating on the *error* rather than velocity. For a stationary target the two are equivalent (`de/dt = −v`), but when the mission advances to the next waypoint the error jumps discontinuously, producing a brief thrust spike *toward* the new target. The HW spool/gimbal limits absorb the spike, but the kick effectively pre-charges the actuator before normal control resumes.

The PID gains (`KP=4.5, KI=0.3, KD=1.5`, integral clamp `MAX_INT=15`) keep the proportional pull stronger than PD's `KP=3.0` and damping just below critical (ζ ≈ 0.35). The lesson encoded here: without modeling assistance (mass + gravity), even a well-tuned PID can be out-performed by a simpler PD that knows the plant.

## Disturbances and noise

A separate top-level `wind` block generates random horizontal gusts that affect both vehicles equally. Both FC blocks also receive their position readings with sensor jitter. The knobs:

- **`FORCE_MAX_PCT` in `wind.ts`** *(default 15%)* — peak gust magnitude as a fraction of max thrust. Each gust picks a random direction in the X–Z plane, a random magnitude up to this limit, and a random duration between **`DURATION_MIN_TICKS`** (1 s) and **`DURATION_MAX_TICKS`** (10 s). When the duration expires, a fresh gust is sampled.
- **`NOISE_PCT` in `fc_pd.ts` and `fc_pid.ts`** *(default 2%)* — uniform per-axis jitter added to the position reading before the control law runs. At 2% (±0.1 m peak) it's barely visible. Crank it past ~20% and the derivative term — especially in the error-derivative form, with its `1/DT` amplification — starts shaking the floater.

What to watch on the **Wind force (N)** chart: a step-like trace as gusts come and go. PD will drift in whichever direction the current gust pushes; PID's integral chases the bias but takes time to catch up before the next gust changes everything. The two knobs interact — noisy sensors make the integral wander, which slows PID's tracking of changing wind. That interaction is most of what real autopilot tuning is about.

## State shape

The state nests both vehicles under a `vehicles` sub-tree so each one carries its own physics and mission independently:

```
vehicles: {
  v1: { pos, vel, acc, thrust: { desired, actual }, mission: { ... } },
  v2: { pos, vel, acc, thrust: { desired, actual }, mission: { ... },
        err: { x, y, z } },          // PID integral accumulator
}
```

Only `v2` carries the PID integral. The two vehicles run independent block instances scoped to their own sub-trees — `hw` and `world` are the same function called twice per tick, once per floater.
