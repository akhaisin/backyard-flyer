# Control blocks

Standalone simulations focused on PID control fundamentals: what each term does, how to tune a controller from first principles, and two automatic tuning algorithms.

---

## [PID tutorial — control fundamentals](#control/pid-tutorial)

A single 1D plant — a mass on a damped surface — driven by a PID controller. Live sliders let you set the target position and adjust every parameter in real time: proportional gain Kp, integral gain Ki, derivative gain Kd, and the plant's own mass and damping.

Work through the terms in order. Start with P alone: the plant moves toward the target but overshoots and oscillates, or stops short with a steady-state error depending on the damping. Add D to introduce braking force proportional to velocity — oscillation drops and the response tightens. Add I to eliminate the residual offset that P alone cannot cancel.

**What it introduces:** the three PID terms in isolation, the trade-off between speed and stability, and why each term is needed.

## [PID auto-tuning — STR](#control/pid-tune-str)

⚠️ *Work in progress.*

A Self-Tuning Regulator that identifies the plant model online using Recursive Least Squares, then derives PID gains automatically via pole placement. No manual tuning required — the controller adapts as it runs.

The RLS estimator fits a second-order plant model to the observed input/output data. Once the estimate has converged, pole placement maps the desired closed-loop poles to a set of PID gains. The simulation shows the identified model parameters alongside the control response so you can watch the estimator converge in real time.

The implementation can drift or get stuck under aggressive setpoint changes. Toggle AUTO/MANUAL to recover, or restart.

**What it introduces:** online system identification, recursive estimation, and model-based gain derivation.

## [PID auto-tuning — Relay-feedback](#control/pid-tune-relay)

Åström and Hägglund's relay-feedback method — the algorithm behind the "auto-tune" button on most industrial PID controllers.

Press *Tune Now*. The controller is temporarily replaced by a bang-bang relay that switches the output between fixed high and low values. The plant responds with a self-sustaining limit cycle. The relay records the oscillation period `Tu` and uses the relay amplitude and the observed output amplitude to compute the ultimate gain `Ku`. Ziegler–Nichols rules then yield Kp, Ki, Kd directly.

The whole procedure takes a few oscillation cycles and requires no plant model knowledge in advance — only that the plant will oscillate when driven by a relay.

**What it introduces:** limit-cycle identification, the Ziegler–Nichols method, and the practical approach used in real industrial auto-tuners.
